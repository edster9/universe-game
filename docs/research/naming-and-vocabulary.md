# How invented things get their names

Surveyed 2026-09-29 for the [vocabulary](../ideas/vocabulary.md) conversation (see also [recognition](../ideas/recognition.md)). The question: where does the name of a player's invention come from, and what happens when two players name the same thing differently, or speak different real languages? Sources are official pages, wikis, interviews, and papers; reviews and guides are marked. Nothing was tested first-hand.

## Summary

Games that let players name things mostly keep two labels: a **type** the engine knows and a **name** the player gave, shown side by side. The first namer's label usually wins for everyone (No Man's Sky), or the name belongs to one object only and the type underneath doesn't change (Minecraft, EVE). Games with several in-world languages handle meeting by garbling or hiding speech, then letting players learn meanings from context over time (World of Warcraft, Heaven's Vault, Chants of Sennaar). Games with players who speak different real languages use a shared dictionary of **concepts** that each viewer sees in their own language (Final Fantasy XIV), or machine translation of free text (Roblox). Research agrees that a shared vocabulary can emerge with no authority, through many small "did you understand me?" exchanges: suddenly, after a period of confusion, and much faster when people meet many different partners than fixed neighbours. Human categories are held as typical examples, recognised by resemblance, which fits naming by showing a thing rather than defining it.

## 1. Players naming what they find or make

**No Man's Sky.** Every planet and species has a procedurally generated default name. The first player to find one may rename it, "within limits set by a content filter", and upload it to the shared server, "having their name credited with the discovery to be seen by other players". For us: first-namer-wins is simple, and rarely contested because players have visited "less than 1%" of planets.
Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No_Man%27s_Sky · https://www.shacknews.com/article/96223/how-to-name-animals-and-plants-in-no-mans-sky

**Spore.** Players name the creatures they design and upload them to the shared Sporepedia, which populates other players' games. For us: a name attached to a *design*, not to each copy.
Source: https://www.awn.com/news/more-250000-spore-creatures-createdand-counting

**EVE Online.** Every ship can be renamed by its owner, and other players' screens can show the "ship name" and the "ship type" as two separate labels. For us: a name for one object beside the game's name for its kind.
Source: https://wiki.eveuniversity.org/Overview_manipulation

**Minecraft.** An anvil renames any item; "any changed name is italicized", so everyone can tell a player's name from the game's. Renaming "does not alter an item's fundamental type or behavior". The game's own names are stored as language-neutral keys (such as `block.minecraft.dirt`) that each player's language file turns into a word. For us: the closest existing match to "laws know concepts, words are a display layer".
Sources: https://minecraft.wiki/w/Mechanics/Anvil · https://docs.minecraftforge.net/en/1.16.x/concepts/internationalization/

**Space Engineers and Kerbal Space Program.** A Space Engineers blueprint is called something "like 'large grid 123'" until renamed, and is shared through Steam Workshop. Kerbal Space Program players share named craft files on the fan site KerbalX, and naming is a hobby (destinations, puns, class prefixes). For us: the designer's name spreads with the file; nothing reconciles two names for similar designs.
Sources: https://spaceengineers.wiki.gg/wiki/Blueprint · https://forum.kerbalspaceprogram.com/topic/182050-out-of-curiosity-whats-your-craft-naming-scheme

**Star Wars Galaxies.** When a crafter turns a good result into a factory schematic, "this option lets you choose the name of all the items made in the factory run". Items from one run share a serial number. For us: the maker names a batch, and a mark ties copies to one making.
Source: https://swg.fandom.com/wiki/Manufacturing (wiki source read through the MediaWiki API)

**Ultima Online.** A Grandmaster crafter who makes an exceptional item may add their "Maker's Mark", so the maker's name is visible to anyone who examines it. For us: the maker's identity travels, not a name for the kind.
Sources: https://www.uoguide.com/Exceptional · https://www.gamebanshee.com/kxuwx (retrospective article)

**Wurm Online.** Items of quality 20 or more carry "the signature of their creator", but "unclear signatures have some letters of the name replaced by a dot"; better quality gives a clearer signature. For us: a mark whose legibility depends on the thing itself.
Source: https://www.wurmpedia.com/index.php/Signature (wiki source read through the MediaWiki API)

**A Tale in the Desert.** The community unlocks technologies by donating materials, and "players can write, introduce, and pass laws", but no evidence was found that players name the technologies. For us: shared invention without player naming.
Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Tale_in_the_Desert · https://atitd.wiki/tale10/Research

## 2. Languages per culture or per mind

**Dwarf Fortress.** Each race has its own language, used for "the names of the universe, continents, rivers ... dwarves, artifacts and engravings". The word lists are fixed, not generated per world: one paper calls the four languages "relexification[s]" of each other, the same concepts with different words. A creature's second name is shown in the native words and "translated to English in several places". For us: shared concepts, a word per culture, translation on demand.
Sources: https://dwarffortresswiki.org/dwarven_language · https://dwarffortresswiki.org/index.php/v0.34:Name · https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-3626/paper3.pdf (Cai and Martens, "Towards Procedural Generation of Constructed Languages for Games")

**World of Warcraft.** Horde and Alliance players each speak a faction language, and nearby speech from the other side is shown garbled. It was added because cross-faction talk tended to be "insulting and profane". Early on the filter didn't change numbers and symbols, so players got messages through anyway until it was patched. For us: a barrier made by rule, which players routed around.
Source: https://warcraft.wiki.gg/wiki/Language

**Heaven's Vault.** The player guesses each ancient word from a short list, and "we won't tell you if you're right or wrong," says its writer Jon Ingold. Meet a word "often enough and she will grow confident in her translation"; sometimes she replaces an earlier guess. For us: meaning learned from repeated context, held with more or less confidence, and revisable.
Sources: https://www.thesixthaxis.com/2018/04/27/interview-talking-with-inkles-about-deciphering-ancient-language-in-heavens-vault/ · https://filmstories.co.uk/reviews/heavens-vault-review/

**Chants of Sennaar.** Each floor of a tower has its own people and language. Guesses in the journal show in a different font until confirmed by matching glyphs to pictures of objects or actions. Bilingual signs link languages, and the goal is to get the peoples talking. For us: showing (pointing at pictures of things) is how words get confirmed, and a translator between groups is itself a role.
Sources: https://thelanguagecloset.com/2024/12/07/playing-chants-of-sennaar/ · https://infinitefreetime.com/tag/tower-of-babel/

**Ultima.** Ultima's runes have "a one-to-one correspondence with the English alphabet": a new script, the same language. For us: flavour, not a barrier.
Source: https://strategywiki.org/wiki/Ultima_V:_Warriors_of_Destiny/Runic_Alphabet_and_Signs

## 3. Players who speak different real languages

**Final Fantasy XIV.** Pressing Tab in chat opens a dictionary of fixed phrases and game terms in 41 categories; each "translates ... into the native language for the viewer", across English, Japanese, French, and German. When data centres merged, English-speaking players were surprised by how differently other groups used these template phrases. For us: a dictionary of *concepts* shown in each viewer's language covers what the game knows, not words players invent.
Sources: https://ffxiv.consolegameswiki.com/wiki/Auto-Translate · https://automaton-media.com/en/news/20220706-13845/

**Roblox.** Since February 2024, chat is machine-translated between 16 languages in about 100 milliseconds by one model, trained with slang that human translators added. For us: free-text translation is practical now, but an invented name has nothing to translate to.
Source: https://about.roblox.com/vi/newsroom/2024/02/breaking-down-language-barriers-with-a-multilingual-translation-model

**Throne and Liberty and Albion Online.** Throne and Liberty puts a translate button on each chat message (German-language report). Albion Online instead gives each language its own chat channels and servers. For us: the two usual answers are translate-on-demand or separate by language.
Sources: https://mein-mmo.de/besonders-gutes-feature-von-throne-and-liberty/ · https://mmorpg.gg/albion-onlines-into-the-fray-patch-6-adds-japanese-and-italian-language-support

## 4. How shared words emerge with no authority

**The naming game (Steels; Baronchelli and others).** Two agents look at the same object. The speaker says a word for it (inventing one if it has none). If the hearer knows that word, both drop every other word for the object. If not, the hearer adds it to their list. In simulations, many names get invented, compete, and then the population agrees suddenly; this "disorder/order transition" gets *sharper* as the population grows. When anyone can meet anyone, the meetings needed, and the peak count of competing words, grow as the population to the power 1.5. In a population spread out in space, with only neighbours meeting, each agent needs to remember less, but agreement "takes longer to complete": local dialects form and their borders drift slowly.
Sources: https://arxiv.org/abs/physics/0509075 (Baronchelli, Felici, Caglioti, Loreto, Steels, "Sharp transition towards shared vocabularies in multi-agent systems") · https://arxiv.org/abs/physics/0512045 (Baronchelli, Dall'Asta, Barrat, Loreto, "Topology induced coarsening in language games")

**The Talking Heads experiment.** From 1999 to 2001, robot cameras in Antwerp, Brussels, Paris, Tokyo, London, and elsewhere played naming games about shapes on a whiteboard. They "created for the first time ever a new shared vocabulary", and humans online could join in and influence it. For us: naming grounded in a shared scene works among agents each with their own camera.
Source: https://langsci-press.org/catalog/book/49

**The same game with people.** Centola and Baronchelli had 24 to 96 people name a picture of a face, paying 50 cents when a pair matched and taking 25 cents when it didn't. Where people always met the same few neighbours, "global social conventions never emerged". Where partners were random each round, a shared name appeared from nothing, reaching 96% of players on average. A follow-up found that a committed minority of about 25% could flip an established name; below that, the majority held.
Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4343158 · https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/20031/ (Centola, Becker, Brackbill, Baronchelli, Science 2018)

**Lewis signalling games (Lewis, Skyrms).** A sender sees a state and sends a signal; a receiver acts; both gain if the act fits. Skyrms showed that signals that begin as "more or less random sounds" become meaningful through simple reinforcement, with no prior agreement. Some runs get stuck in partial systems where one signal covers several states.
Source: https://ndpr.nd.edu/reviews/signals-evolution-learning-and-information

**Emergent communication in AI agents.** Lazaridou and Baroni's survey finds that neural agents do invent working codes, but these are hard to read, and agents develop only "a code that is sufficient to solve the task at hand". Their main problem is "language drift", where task pressures push the code away from human language unless it's anchored to it.
Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.02419

**In plain terms.** Agreement comes from many small exchanges about a thing both can see: success reinforces a word, failure spreads it. Mixing speeds it up; fixed neighbourhoods make dialects. Nobody has to decide.

## 5. How human categories work

Eleanor Rosch's experiments found that people hold a category as its typical members: the most typical have "most attributes in common with other members of the category and least attributes in common with other categories". Membership is a matter of degree, recognised by resemblance, not by a checklist. The main objection: people reason past looks, so a dog altered to look like a raccoon is still a dog. For us: a word can point at a remembered example, and a new thing gets the word if it looks and works enough like it.
Sources: https://doi.org/10.1016/0010-0285(75)90024-9 (Rosch and Mervis 1975) · https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/concepts/

## What this suggests

- Most games found separate what a thing *is* (a type the engine knows) from what it's *called* (a label someone gave).
- Where one shared label is needed, games pick one rule: first namer wins (No Man's Sky), each object's owner names it (EVE, Minecraft), or the maker names the batch (Star Wars Galaxies). None reconcile two independent names for the same kind of thing.
- Real-language barriers are handled with a concept dictionary shown in each viewer's language (FFXIV, Minecraft's keys) or machine translation (Roblox). Invented names fall outside both.
- In-world language barriers are made by rule (WoW) and crossed by learning from context, with confidence that grows and can be revised (Heaven's Vault, Chants of Sennaar).
- Research says a shared vocabulary emerges on its own through repeated grounded exchanges: suddenly, faster with mixing, as regional dialects with fixed neighbours. A committed quarter can change an established word.
- Prototype theory suggests a name can point at a remembered example, with new things matched by resemblance, which suits naming by showing.
